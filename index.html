First, The Thing You Are Right About
We are not going to sell you something that does not fit

You told our team that you are a supplier — that you handle enquiries for the people who do the paint jobs — and that most of what a marketing agency does therefore cannot help you. That is a fair read, and on the usual version of the pitch you are correct.

Chasing homeowners would be wrong for you. Your customers are contractors, applicators, facilities teams and specifiers. They already know coatings. They are not going to be persuaded by an advert.

So everything below is about three things only: the online counter you have already built and not switched on, the specification work you already do and cannot measure, and the 27 years of proof sitting on your site under a filename that says “test”.

Nothing in here asks you to become something you are not, and nothing in here is an advert.

You told our team how this actually works. A firm with a job to do comes to you for the supplies. You work out the system, how much material it needs and how long it will take, and you quote it. They order against your quote, and you are paid when the job is finished.

That estimate is your product. Twenty-seven years of knowing how much paint a job actually takes is not something a contractor can get from a website or a catalogue, and it is the reason they come to you rather than ordering direct.

So the important number in your business is not visits or enquiries. It is: how many quotes did you issue, and how many turned into orders? That is the one figure that tells you whether the estimating work is paying for itself.

Nothing you own records it. Not the website, which cannot see an enquiry arrive. Not the enquiry form, which asks for a message and nothing a quote needs. A quote that goes out and never comes back simply stops existing.

This is the finding that matters most, and it is the one with nothing to look at — which is exactly the problem. Everything below is visible on your website. This is not, and it is worth more than all of it.

Why this is worth raising before anything else

A contractor who asked you to quote a job has already decided they need the work done. They are not a stranger and they are not browsing. They are the warmest enquiry any business gets.

Some of those quotes were lost on price. Some were lost because the job was delayed and nobody rang back. You cannot currently tell those two apart, and they need completely different answers.

The whole till is fitted and the drawer is empty. You have paid for a full ecommerce system, catalogued 571 products across 27 categories, and a customer who finds a paint brush on your site cannot buy it.

Look at what is actually in that catalogue. Contractor brushes. Wire cage roller frames. Chipping hammers. Brown paper rolls. Disposable overalls. Drill mixing paddles. Squeegee holders. These are consumables. The same contractor buys them again next month, and the month after.

That is the easiest revenue in your business and the least worth a phone call. A contractor ordering forty pounds of brushes has to ring your counter, and somebody on your team has to stop what they are doing and take that order by hand.

And the part we would deliberately leave alone

The coatings themselves should stay an enquiry. Jotun and Remmers systems need a specification, a substrate and a conversation with your team. Nobody should be buying those from a dropdown, and we are not suggesting it.

The tools and consumables are not that — and they are the ones already listed on your site with no price on them.

What These Four Things Actually Are

In plain terms, with no jargon. Four things come up in this document. None of them is advertising, and none of them requires a new website. Here is exactly what each one is, what would change on a Tuesday morning, and what it deliberately does not do.

1 · A list of every job you have quoted

What it is: one list, with every quote on it and a stage against each — enquiry, site survey, quote sent, chasing, won, lost. Each one carries the customer, the job and the value. When a quote has sat unanswered for a fortnight, it tells somebody to ring them.

What changes on a Tuesday morning: you can look at one screen and say “we have £X of quotes out, this many are older than three weeks, and nobody has rung these four”. At the end of the year you can say how many you issued and how many came back — which is the number that tells you whether the estimating is paying for itself.

What it does not do: it does not write your quotes and it does not replace whatever you produce them on now. Your existing system makes the quote and sends it. This is only about what happens after it leaves — the part that is usually nowhere.

2 · An estimating page — saying out loud what you already do

What it is: one page on your website, and a better enquiry form. The page says plainly that you will work out the system, the quantities and the coverage for a job. The form asks the four things an estimate actually needs — what is being coated, roughly how big, what condition it is in, and when it starts — instead of asking for a message.

What changes on a Tuesday morning: an enquiry arrives with enough on it to start pricing, rather than starting with a phone call to find out what the job even is. And a contractor searching for someone to work out a specification finds a page that says you do that. Today there is no such page.

What it does not do: it does not price anything automatically and it does not put a calculator on your website. The estimate is still done by your people, because that judgement is the thing being sold.

3 · The trade counter — switching on the shop you already own

What it is: putting a price and an order button on the tools and consumables only — brushes, rollers, overalls, masking, paddles — behind a login, so each account sees its own agreed price rather than a public one.

This is not building a shop. The shop is already installed on your website. The cart page works, the account page works, and all 571 products are already catalogued. It is configuration and trade pricing, not a new build, which is why it is a far smaller job than it sounds.

What changes on a Tuesday morning: a contractor orders his brushes at six in the morning from the van, priced on his account, and nobody on your counter stops what they are doing to write it down. He can also see what he ordered last month and repeat it.

What it does not do: it does not sell coatings. Every Jotun, Remmers and Flexcrete system stays an enquiry, exactly as it is today, because those need a substrate, a specification and one of your people. That is your advantage and we would not touch it.

4 · Analytics — being able to see your own website

What it is: a small free tag from Google added to your website. It takes about twenty minutes and there is nothing to buy.

What changes on a Tuesday morning: you can see which of your eight brands people come looking for, which of your 27 categories they browse and then leave, and which of your 571 products nobody has opened in a year. For a firm carrying stock, that is purchasing information rather than marketing information — it tells you what to hold and what to stop holding.

What it does not do: it does not advertise anything, it does not cost anything, and it does not identify individual people. It only starts recording from the day it goes on, which is the one reason not to leave it any longer.

Fix First — This Week, And All Of It Is Free
  • Start writing quotes down in one place — issued, value, won or lost, and why. A spreadsheet will do to begin with. Nothing else in this document matters as much.
  • Install analytics — free, and it starts counting the day it goes on. Everything else is guesswork until this exists.
  • Rename the three “test” case study addresses — ten minutes, and it stops a royal residence being filed as a placeholder
  • Put Royal Hillsborough Castle on the homepage — your strongest single reference, currently three clicks deep
  • Ask your best twenty contractors for a Google review — 27 years of goodwill and nothing published
Build Next
  • A quote pipeline — every estimate recorded, with a reminder to chase the ones that have gone quiet, so a won job and a forgotten one stop looking the same
  • An enquiry form that asks what a quote needs — job type, substrate, area, timescale — so the estimate starts before the phone call rather than after it
  • Switch on prices and ordering for tools and consumables only — every coating stays an enquiry, exactly as today
  • A trade account login — agreed contractor prices behind a sign-in, which is how your customers already expect to buy
  • A real page for CPD presentations and specifications, written for architects and engineers rather than contractors
  • Capture on the brochure library, so you know which specifier took which system and can follow it up
  • More case studies — you have 27 years of them and four published

The order matters. Analytics first, because it costs nothing and every decision after it is better informed. The shop second, because it is already built and paid for. Everything else can wait until those two are earning.
