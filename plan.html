The Four Levers, In The Order We Would Pull Them
Lever 1 — Recover the quotes you already issued

No new demand required. No capacity risk. Start here.

Every firm that asked you to price a job had already decided the work needed doing. The estimating time is already spent. Some of those quotes were lost on price and some were lost because the job slipped and nobody rang back — and today you cannot tell those two apart, because nothing records that a quote ever happened.

Those two causes need opposite responses. One is a pricing conversation. The other is a phone call that never got made. Recovering even a portion of the second costs you nothing in capacity, because those jobs were already yours to lose.

Lever 2 — Steer the mix toward the 50% end

The single biggest lever in the business, and it is currently unmanaged.

You told us margin ranges from 20% to 50%. That spread is enormous. The same £500k of revenue is worth £100k or £250k of gross profit depending entirely on which jobs it comes from.

Nothing currently records which jobs sit at which end, so the mix is whatever walks through the door. Once quotes carry a value and an outcome, you can see the pattern — by job type, by customer, by product — and quote deliberately toward the profitable end. Same capacity. More money. No extra jobs.

Lever 3 — Give your counter its time back

This is the one that attacks the operational constraint directly.

Every small repeat order — brushes, rollers, masking, overalls, paddles — taken by hand at the counter is time your people are not spending on the thing you actually sell, which is working out systems and quantities. That estimating judgement is your product. The brush order is not.

Moving predictable repeat consumables off the counter is not an ecommerce project. It is capacity recovery — and capacity is the exact thing you told us is stopping you. It is how a business grows 25% without hiring 25% more people.

Coatings stay an enquiry, permanently. Every Jotun, Remmers and Flexcrete system needs a substrate, a specification and one of your people on the phone. We would not touch that, and nobody should be buying those from a dropdown.

Lever 4 — Be upstream of the work, not bidding for it

Longest to pay off, and the one that gets you to £3M rather than £2.5M.

You already offer CPD presentations, site surveys, specifications and demonstrations. In the specification trade that is not marketing collateral — it is the whole route to market. The specifier writes your system into the document, and then every contractor bidding that job has to come to you.

That is being upstream of the work instead of competing for it. And it selects for exactly the jobs you want: larger, specified, higher margin, planned in advance rather than urgent.

Today there is no page on your website for surveys, specifications, demonstrations or CPD. They are four words in a menu. An architect searching for CPD on protective coatings finds nothing of yours.

Why Northern Ireland Being Small Is An Advantage
Your market can be listed by name

You told us you sell into Northern Ireland only. That is normally described as a limitation. For what you sell, it is the opposite.

Every organisation in Northern Ireland that could ever buy protective coatings can be written down: the commercial and industrial contractors, the architecture practices, the engineering consultancies, the facilities teams, the councils, the housing associations. It is a countable set, not an anonymous audience.

That means coverage, not advertising. You do not need to pay to be found by people you could simply write to, ring, or present a CPD to. And you can know, at any moment, what percentage of the total addressable market in this country you have actually spoken to — which is a number almost no business can calculate.

The Eight-Week Plan
The Phases — Both Halves, In Order
mdash; Both Halves, Two Tracks

There are two pieces of work here. The system that catches and chases the demand you already have, and the advertising that goes after the specification ground none of your competitors have taken. They are not alternatives — the second one cannot run until the first one exists, because there is nowhere to send a click and no way to tell whether it worked. Every phase has a gate on it, and you can stop at any one of them.

Two tracks, running at the same time

The two halves have almost no dependency on each other until the very end, so they do not need to be done one after the other. Track A is the system. Track B is the specification ground and the advertising. They run in parallel for the first month and only meet in week five, when the campaigns need somewhere to land and something to be measured by.

Weeks 1–2 · Both tracks start immediately

Track A — measurement on, day one. Free tag, twenty minutes, nothing to buy. It only records from the day it is switched on, so it goes on before anything is agreed about the rest.

Track A — the quote list built. Stages, values, and the automatic chase when a quote goes quiet. Plus the estimating page and the enquiry form that asks what an estimate actually needs.

Track B — the keyword report pulled. This costs nothing and needs no data from you, so there is no reason at all to wait for it. If Northern Ireland does not search for these terms often enough, we find out in week one rather than week ten and the paid half comes off the table before anybody has spent a pound.

Track B — specification and CPD pages built alongside the system, not after it. They are pages on a site that already works.

Weeks 3–4 · In use, and the back catalogue opens

Track A — training, and the list goes live. We run it alongside your people rather than handing over a login.

Track A — the back catalogue starts. Every quote issued and never heard back on. No new demand, no capacity risk, and the estimating time is already spent. This is where the plan pays for itself first.

Track B — the brochure library set up so you know which specifier downloaded what, before the tender goes out.

Gate: the list is genuinely being used by your team. If it is not, we stop and fix that before anything else — nothing downstream works without it.

Weeks 5–6 · The campaigns go live, small

Campaign 1 — specification and survey. Building owners, facilities managers, architects and engineers. Offering a free site survey and a written specification, not paint. Not one of your five competitors offers this, and only one of them is buying search at all.

Campaign 2 — competitor displacement. Brand terms for the three English websites, geo-restricted to Northern Ireland, small budget.

Campaign 3 — CPD coverage of the Northern Ireland practice list. Direct first, because the list is finite and can be written down.

Gate: the keyword report from week one said there was enough demand. If it did not, this fortnight does not happen and Track B becomes CPD coverage only — which costs nothing in media.

Weeks 7–8 · First real read, and the conditional decisions

First margin review. Which job types, customers and products sit at the 50% end and which at 20% — and what that says about what to quote for.

First campaign read. Surveys booked and specifications written. Anything not producing them gets paused rather than nursed.

The trade counter decision. The pricing question is settled — no retail prices, trade accounts only, trade quantities. What is left is whether the quote data says the counter time is worth reclaiming.

Named-account coverage of the Northern Ireland contractor, specifier and facilities list begins.

What compressing to eight weeks actually costs you — said plainly

Two things genuinely cannot be compressed, and we would rather tell you now than explain it in week seven.

  • The advertising read at eight weeks is early, not final. A specification enquiry can take weeks to become a survey and longer to become an order. At week eight you will see surveys booked; you will not yet see how many turned into material orders. The real judgement is still at ninety days, and we will say so rather than declare victory early.
  • The margin picture at eight weeks is directional. It needs enough quotes to have flowed through to show a pattern. It will be a first read, not a conclusion.

And one deliberate trade-off: in the four-month version, the back catalogue ran before any advertising, so you learned what a recovered quote was worth before spending on a new one. Running them in parallel loses that. We think it is an acceptable trade because the paid budget is small by design and the keyword report gates it in week one — but it is your call, and it is the one real cost of going faster.

What We Are Deliberately Not Doing
Said plainly, so there is no confusion later
  • No volume advertising, and no Meta or Instagram at all. The only paid work proposed starts in week five — specification-led search, aimed at specifiers rather than contractors, and only if a free keyword report justifies it.
  • No advertising of coating systems on price. You would lose that to an English website with a shop, and winning it would be worse — it is the low-margin end.
  • No spend before measurement exists. Advertising into a website that cannot see a conversion is the most common and most expensive mistake we see.
  • No new website. Yours is well built and fast on a phone. Everything here works with it.
  • Nothing that touches your stock or accounts systems. Not replaced, not migrated. If a piece of work would require that, we would tell you and stop rather than proceed.
  • No selling of coating systems online. Ever. Those stay an enquiry.
  • No chasing homeowners. Your customers are contractors, applicators, facilities teams and specifiers.
  • No growth past the ceiling you set. You said doubling would be too much. We have planned to £2.5–3M, not beyond it.
So What Are We Actually Delivering?
In one sentence

First we build the system that catches and chases every quote you send out, and run it with you. Then — only if the data says it is worth it — we go after the specification work none of your competitors are claiming.
